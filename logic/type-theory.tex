\msection{1}{Combinator Calculus}

\msubsection{1.1}{Combinator Algebra}

\begin{definition}{1.1}[terms, and combinators]
  Fix two \textit{constants}, $\cK$ and $\cS$, and a stock of \textit{variables} $x, y, z, f, g, \dots$. The
  \textit{terms} are defined inductively:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Each constant, $\cK$ and $\cS$, is a term.
    \item Each variable $x$ is a term.
    \item If $M$ and $N$ are terms, then $(M \, N)$ is a term: the \textit{application} of $M$ to $N$.
    \item Nothing else is a term.
  \end{enumerate}
  A term without any variables is a \textit{combinator}.

  In one line, with $M$ and $N$ standing for terms already built, \[M,N \Coloneq \cK \, | \, \cS \, | \, x \, | \, (M \, N)\]

  Writing out every bracket gets tiresome, so we adopt the convention where \textbf{application groups to the left}.
  Thus, $\cK \, x \, y$ means $((\cK \, x) y)$ and we only write brackets when the grouping is \textit{not} to the left:
  $f \, (g \, x)$ and $f \, g \, x$ are different terms.

  On their own, terms just sit there. We allow them exactly one type of action, \textit{rewriting}: we write $M \leadsto
  N$ to say $M$ \textit{steps to} $N$, or one rewrite turns $M$ into $N$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{1.2}[reduction]
  The relation $M \leadsto N$, is given by one of the rules:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Rule $\cK$ has nothing above the bar, so it holds outright. $\cK$ keeps the first of two arguments and drops
    the second.
    \begin{prooftree}
      \AxiomC{}
      \RightLabel{\scriptsize $\cK$}
      \UnaryInfC{$((\cK \, P) \, Q) \;\leadsto\; P$}
    \end{prooftree}

    \item Rule $\cS$ also has nothing above the bar, so it holds outright. $\cS$ hands its third argument to both of the
    others and applies the first result to the second.
    \begin{prooftree}
      \AxiomC{}
      \RightLabel{\scriptsize $\cS$}
      \UnaryInfC{$(((\cS \, P) \, Q) \, R) \;\leadsto\; ((P \, R) \, (Q \, R))$}
    \end{prooftree}

    \item Rule (app\textsubscript{1}) says that if $P \leadsto P'$ then $P \, Q \leadsto P' \, Q$: a step in the
    function is a step of the application.
    \begin{prooftree}
      \AxiomC{$P \leadsto P'$}
      \RightLabel{\scriptsize $\text{app}_1$}
      \UnaryInfC{$(P \, Q) \;\leadsto\; (P' \, Q)$}
    \end{prooftree}

    \item Rule (app\textsubscript{2}) says the same of the argument.
    \begin{prooftree}
      \AxiomC{$Q \leadsto Q'$}
      \RightLabel{\scriptsize $\text{app}_2$}
      \UnaryInfC{$(P \, Q) \;\leadsto\; (P \, Q')$}
    \end{prooftree}
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{example}{1.4}[the identity] Here is the run of \textsf{I} on an argument:
  \[
    \begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\qquad}l}
      \cS \, \cK \, \cK \, x & \leadsto \cK \, x \, (\cK \, x)
        & \text{the $\cS$ step, with $P = Q = \cK$ and $R = x$} \\
                             & \leadsto x
        & \text{the $\cK$ step: keep $x$, drop $\cK \, x$.}
    \end{array}
  \]
  The function that returns its argument unchanged isn't one of the two constants, but the language has it all the same.
  Whatever $x$ is, $\mathsf{I} \, x \leadsto^* x$.
\end{example}

Terms built from constants by application, together with rules for rewriting them, make a \textit{combinatory algebra},
and $\cK$ and $\cS$ are its basic combinators.

\msubsection{1.2}{Confluence}
The rules (app\textsubscript{1}) and (app\textsubscript{2}) let a step happen anywhere inside a term, so a term can
often step in more than one way. A \textbf{redex}, short for \textit{reducible expression}, is a term with the shape of
the left of $(\cK)$ or $(\cS)$:
\[\cK \, P \, Q \qquad \qquad \cS \, P \, Q \, R\]
What the rule puts in its place is the \textbf{reduct}. A step rewrites exactly one redex.

\begin{definition}{1.6}[Church--Rosser; on loan]
If $M \leadsto^* N_1$ and $M \leadsto^* N_2$, then there is a term $P$ such that $N_1 \leadsto^* P$ and $N_2 \leadsto^*
P$. It's usually remembered as a picture. The two runs at the top are given; the theorem promises the two dashed runs at
the bottom.
\begin{center}
  \begin{tikzpicture}[
      node distance = 1.6cm,
      red/.style = {-{Stealth[length=1.8mm]}, decorate,
                    decoration={snake, amplitude=.35mm, segment length=2.2mm,
                                post length=1.2mm}},
      exists/.style = {-{Stealth[length=1.8mm]}, dashed}
    ]
    \node (M)  at (0,0)       {$M$};
    \node (N1) at (-1.6,-1.6) {$N_1$};
    \node (N2) at (1.6,-1.6)  {$N_2$};
    \node (P)  at (0,-3.2)    {$P$};

    \draw[red]    (M)  -- node[above left]  {\scriptsize $*$} (N1);
    \draw[red]    (M)  -- node[above right] {\scriptsize $*$} (N2);
    \draw[exists] (N1) -- node[below left]  {\scriptsize $*$} (P);
    \draw[exists] (N2) -- node[below right] {\scriptsize $*$} (P);
  \end{tikzpicture}
\end{center}
This property is called \textit{confluence}. Some terms can step in more than one way; others can't step at all. A term
with no redex is in \textit{normal form}. Machines waiting for inputs, such as $\cK \, x$ and $\cS \, x \, y$, and
$\cK, \cS$ and variables on their own, are all in normal form.
\end{definition}

\msubsection{1.3}{Evaluation Strategies}
An implementation of the combinator calculus has to decide which redex to rewrite when a term has more than one. The
choice can't change the result, but it can change how much work it takes. Two strategies matter:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Strict, or eager}. Rewrite the \textbf{innermost} redex first, one with no redex inside it, and work
  outwards. In programming terms: run the arguments, before the call.
  \item \textit{Lazy}. Rewrite the \textbf{outermost} redex first, one that sits inside no other, work inwards. In
  programming terms, run an argument only when the function needs it.
\end{enumerate}
The default is a bet on which behaviour we want more often:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textit{Predictable cost favours strict}. The work for an argument is paid at the call, so we can read the cost
  off the line. A lazy sum of a long list can pile up a million unrun additions.

  \item \textit{Effects favour strict}. Printing, reading, raising an error happen in the order written. Lazily, an
  effect happens when its result is demanded.

  \item \textit{Modularity favours lazy}. One part of a program describes an enormous list; another takes what it needs.
  Python's generators borrow the idea.
\end{enumerate}
Everything in this section assumes that the run ends, and confluence says that two runs of a term can be brought back
together without saying anything about either stopping.

\msubsection{1.4}{Programming}
Running is plainly computing: a term goes in, a normal form comes out.
\begin{definition}{1.11}[definitions]
  A \textit{definition} is an equation \[f \, x_1 \dots x_n \coloneqq M, \] where:
  \begin{itemize}
    \item $f$ is a new name;
    \item $x_1, \dots, x_n$ are distinct variables;
    \item $M$ is a term built from constants, names defined earlier, and the variables $x_1, \dots, x_n$ alone.
  \end{itemize}
  Once defined, the name $f$ may be used as a term, and it steps by the rule
  \begin{prooftree}
    \AxiomC{}
    \RightLabel{\scriptsize def}
    \UnaryInfC{$((\cdots((f \, P_1) \, P_2) \cdots) \, P_n) \;\leadsto\; M
      \text{ with } P_i \text{ for each } x_i$}
  \end{prooftree}
  alongside the rules of Definition 1.2.

  The rule (def) says that wherever $f$ stands with $n$ arguments, it may be replaced by the right-hand side, with the
  arguments written in for the parameters; with (app\textsubscript{1}) and (app\textsubscript{2}), this can happen
  anywhere in a term. The \textsf{I} above is now official with definition $\cS \, \cK \, \cK$ and no parameters, and
  three more are useful:
  \begin{align*}
    \mathit{twice} \, f \, x     &\coloneqq f \, (f \, x), \\
    \mathit{comp} \, f \, g \, x &\coloneqq f \, (g \, x), \\
    \mathit{flip} \, f \, x \, y &\coloneqq f \, y \, x.
  \end{align*}
\end{definition}

\begin{theorem}{1.16}[two constants suffice]
  Every definition by equations can be replaced by a combinator in $\cK$ and $\cS$ alone that runs the same way on every
  argument.
\end{theorem}

\begin{theorem}{1.19}[Turing completeness]
  The functions on numerals that combinators in $\cK$ and $\cS$ compute are
  exactly the functions that a Turing machine computes.

  Recursion is the one thing the definition seems to lack. No name may occur in its own definition, but a function may
  be applied to itself, and that is enough. Define
  \[\mathit{self} \, f \, x \coloneq f \, (x \, x) \qquad \text{and} \qquad \mathsf{Y} \, f \coloneq \mathit{self} \, f
  \, (\mathit{self}\, f),\]neither of which mentions itself, and run \textsf{Y} on any $f$:
  \[
    \begin{array}{r@{\;}l@{\qquad}l}
      \mathsf{Y} \, f & \leadsto \mathit{self} \, f \, (\mathit{self} \, f)
        & \mathsf{Y} \\
                      & \leadsto f \, (\mathit{self} \, f \, (\mathit{self} \, f))
        & \text{$\mathit{self}$, with $\mathit{self} \, f$ for $x$.}
    \end{array}
  \]
  Write $\Theta$ for $\mathit{self} \, f (\mathit{self} \, f)$. The second line says that $\Theta \leadsto f \, \Theta$:
  $\Theta$ hands $f$ a copy of itself, and that copy, when it is run, hands $f$ another, as many times as the run asks
  for. A function that is to call itself takes the copy as an extra first parameter, and \textsf{Y} supplies it.
\end{theorem}

We've taken a lot away from our idea of a program. What's left:
\begin{itemize}
  \item A program is a finite list of definitions and a term to evaluate.
  \item A definition is an equation.
  \item To run the term is to replace, over and over, a left-hand side by its right-hand side, until no replacement is
  left to make.
  \item We have no store, no stack, no instruction pointer. The order of steps cannot change the answer, although it may
  change whether we get one at all.
\end{itemize}

\msubsection{1.5}{The Monster}
Confluence promises that a term has at most one normal form, but does not promise that there is a guaranteed one.
Nothing in Definition 1.1 says what may be applied to what, so a function can be applied to itself. Consider definition
\[\omega x \coloneq x \, x\]
It is the function that applies its argument to itself. Now, apply $\omega$ to itself,
\[\omega\omega \leadsto \omega \omega \leadsto \omega \omega \leadsto \dots\]
This is the Monster: a legal construction, the loop of \textsf{Y} with nothing inside it. $\mathit{self}$ \textsf{I} does
what $\omega$ does, and \textsf{Y I} runs forever.

\msubsection{1.6}{Types}
\begin{definition}{1.21}[types]
  Fix a stock of \textit{basic types}, such as \textit{Int}, \textit{String} and \textit{Bool}; write $p$, $q$, $r$, for
  them. The types are then inductively defined:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item Every basic type $p$ is a type.
    \item If $A$ and $B$ are types, $A \to B$ is a type: the \textit{function type}, the type of functions that take an
    $A$ and return a $B$.
    \item Nothing else is a type.
  \end{enumerate}

  In one line, \[A, B \Coloneqq p \, | \, (A \to B)\]

  Read $A \to B$ as a contract. So, $\textit{Int} \to \textit{Bool}$ is the type of a test on integers, $(\textit{Int}
  \to \textit{Int}) \to \textit{Int}$ is the type of something that takes a function, and $(p \to q) \to p$ is a type
  too, whatever the basic types of $p$ and $q$.

  Note that unlike terms which are grouped to the left, for types we group to the \textit{right}.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{1.22}[context and judgement]
  A \textit{context} $\Gamma$ is a finite list pairing each variable in scope with a type, none listed twice:
  \[\Gamma = x_1 : A_1, \dots, x_n : A_n\]
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{1.23}[judgement]
  A \textit{judgement} is a claim
  \[\Gamma \vdash_{\text{KS}} M : A,\]
  read ``$M$ has a type $A$ when its variables have the types $\Gamma$ gives them''. When $\Gamma$ is empty, it is
  written $\vdash_{\text{KS}} M : A$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{1.24}[typing]
  The two constants have the type schemas
  \begin{align}
    &(K) \qquad \cK : A \to B \to A \\
    &(S) \qquad \cS: (A \to B \to C) \to (A \to B) \to A \to C
  \end{align}
  where $A$, $B$ and $C$ stand for arbitrary types: each line is a \textit{schema}, and gives one type for each choice of
  letters.

  Let $\Gamma$ be a context, $M$ a term and $A$ a type. A \textit{typing} of $\Gamma \vdash_{\text{KS}} M : A$ is a
  finite sequence of judgements
  \[M_1: A_1, M_2:A_2, \dots, M_n : A_n\]
  whose last judgement $M_n:A_n$ is $M:A$, and in which each judgement $M_k : A_k$, read ``$M_k$ has type $A_k$ in
  context $\Gamma$'', is justified in one of four ways:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item (K) $M_k$ is $\cK$, and $A_k$ is an instance of (1.1);
    \item (S) $M_k$ is $\cS$, and $A_k$ is an instance of (1.2);
    \item (\textit{var}) $M_k$ is variable $x$ (or a ready-made constant), and $x:A_k$ is declared in
    $\Gamma$;
    \item (\textit{app}) there are two earlier judgements $M_i : A_i$ and $M_j:A_j$, with $i, j < k$, such that $A_i$
    is the type $A_j \to A_k$ and $M_k$ is the term $M_i \, M_j$.
  \end{enumerate}
  A typing whose last line is $M:A$ \textbf{witnesses} $\Gamma \vdash_{\text{KS}} M:A$.
\end{definition}

\msubsection{1.7}{Subject Reduction}

\begin{definition}{1.29}[subject reduction]
  If $\Gamma \vdash_{\text{KS}} M:A$ and $M \leadsto N$ by a $\cK$ step, an $\cS$ step or a ready-made step, then
  $\Gamma \vdash_{\text{KS}} N : A$. (check course notes for proof)
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{1.30}[normalisation]
  If $\Gamma \vdash_{\text{KS}} M : A$, then $M$ has a normal form.
  
  This bans the Monster and all its kin. $\Omega$ has no normal form, so it has \textbf{no typing}; nor has any term
  with $\Omega$ inside it. However, some programs that do stop are refused too: $\cK \, x \, \Omega$ has a normal form
  and no typing.
\end{definition}

\msubsection{2.1}{Formulas, Axioms and Proofs}

\begin{definition}{2.1}[formulas] Fix a stock of \textit{propositional variables} $p, q, r, \dots$, called
  \textit{letters} for short. The \textit{formulas} are defined inductively:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item every letter is a formula;
    \item if $\varphi$ and $\psi$ are formulas, so is $\varphi \to \psi$;
    \item nothing else is a formula.
  \end{enumerate}
  In one line, \[\varphi, \psi \Coloneq p \, | \, \varphi \to \psi\]
  with the arrow grouping to the right, so that $\varphi \to \psi \to \chi$ is $\varphi \to (\psi \to \chi)$.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{2.3}[axioms]
  The \textit{axioms} are all instances of the two schemas
  \begin{align*}
    &(\text{I}) \qquad \varphi \to \psi \to \varphi \\
    &(\text{II}) \qquad (\varphi \to \psi \to \chi) \to (\varphi \to \psi) \to \varphi \to \chi
  \end{align*}
  where $\varphi, \psi$ and $\chi$ stand for arbitrary formulas.
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{2.4}[proof from hypotheses]
  Let $\Gamma$ be a set of formulas, the \textit{hypotheses}. A \textit{proof of $\varphi$ from $\Gamma$} is a finite
  sequence of formulas \[\varphi_1, \varphi_2, \dots, \varphi_n\]
  whose last formula $\varphi_n$ is $\varphi$, and in which each formula $\varphi_k$ is justified in one of three ways:
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item (\textit{axiom}) $\varphi_k$ is an axiom;
    \item (\textit{hypothesis}) $\varphi_k$ is a member of $\Gamma$;
    \item (\textit{modus ponens}) there are two earlier formulas $\varphi_i$ and $\varphi_j$, with $i, j < k$, such that
    $\varphi_i$ is the formula $\varphi_j \to \varphi_k$.
  \end{enumerate}
\end{definition}

\begin{definition}{2.5}[provability; theorem]
  Let $\Gamma$ be a set of formulas and $\varphi$ a formula. If there is a proof of $\varphi$ from $\Gamma$, we write 
  $\Gamma \vdash_\text{HJ} \varphi$ and say that the proof \textit{witnesses} $\Gamma \vdash_\text{HJ} \varphi$. A
  formula $\varphi$ with $\emptyset \vdash_{\text{HJ}} \varphi$, written $\vdash_{\text{HJ}} \varphi$, is a
  \textit{theorem}.

  Set a proof out as a box, with hypotheses $\Gamma$ above the line and the formulas below it, one to a numbered line,
  each with its justification on the right:

  \begin{deduction}[\Gamma]
    \dl{\varphi_1}{reason}
    \dl{\varphi_2}{reason}
  \end{deduction}
\end{definition}

\begin{lemma}{2.7}[identity]
  For every formula $\varphi$, $\vdash_\text{HJ} \, \varphi \to \varphi$. \\
  \textit{Proof}. The following proof, from no hypotheses, witnesses the claim for a propositional letter $p$. The
  square brackets give the formulas put for the schema's Greek letters, in the order $\varphi, \psi, \chi$.
  \begin{deduction}[\textit{no hypotheses}]
    \dl{(p \to (p \to p) \to p) \to (p \to (p \to p)) \to p \to p}{(II) $[p, p\to p, p]$}
    \dl{p \to (p \to p) \to p}{(I) $[p, p \to p]$}
    \dl{(p \to (p \to p)) \to p \to p}{MP 1, 2}
    \dl{p \to (p \to p)}{(I) $[p, p]$}
    \dl{p \to p}{MP 3, 4}
  \end{deduction}
  
  Lines 1, 2, 4 are matched against the two shapes, lines 3, 5 against the two lines they cite. Nothing in the five
  lines depends on $p$, so same lines with $\varphi$ in place of $p$ prove $\varphi \to \varphi$ for every formula
  $\varphi$. $\square$

  It turns out that \textbf{the thinking runs backwards from the goal}.
  \begin{enumerate}
    \item The goal $p \to p$ is not an axiom, so it comes by modus ponens: from some $\chi$ and $\chi \to (p \to p)$.
    \item Could $\chi \to (p \to p)$ be an instance of (I)? Then $\chi$ would be $p$, and we would need a proof of $p$
    on its own. There cannot be one, nothing in the system proves a lone letter.
    \item So try (II): Its last part, $\varphi \to \chi$, must be $p \to p$. That fixes $\varphi = \chi = p$ and leaves
    $\psi$ free.
    \item The two premises are then $p \to (\psi \to p)$ and $p \to \psi$. Choose $\psi \coloneq p \to p$, and both
    premises are instances of (I).
  \end{enumerate}
\end{lemma}
